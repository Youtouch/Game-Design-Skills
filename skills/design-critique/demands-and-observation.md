# Diagnose demands and choose informative observations

Use decomposition to locate a problem before increasing difficulty, adding tutorial text, or equalizing roles. The procedure below is an original synthesis informed by the craft and research identified in [sources.md](sources.md); it is not an official RGD/RLD table.

## Separate demands

For a disputed interaction, identify what the player must perceive, infer, choose, execute, and recover from. Name the adjustable property, its units where meaningful, and what the model omits. Changing an execution window may leave an inference problem untouched. Completing a scene through an alternate route does not establish use of the skill the scene was intended to teach.

Synthetic example: a wind bridge asks travelers to interpret flags, select a safe interval, then hold two controls together. More time may aid execution but does not distinguish similar flag colors or remove a simultaneous-input barrier. Redundant flag shapes and toggle controls address different demands. Check with affected participants whether they preserve the chosen challenge and support the participants' own goals. Do not equate disability with inexperience or require everyone to prefer the same challenge.

**Counterexample and exception:** a bypass may undermine a claim that the bridge teaches timing, yet enrich a game about free exploration. Preserve it if that freedom is part of the brief; assess timing transfer separately when timing is actually required later. Likewise, an intentionally obscure clue can support deduction, while an obscure input may prevent participation without adding useful inference.

For creative variation, relate a small set of verbs to object properties and follow consequences across actions. In a paper theater, folding changes rigidity, clipping anchors an edge, and fanning moves loose surfaces. Their combinations can create a ramp or reveal a scene. More combinations are not automatically better. A purely decorative stamp might serve authorship even when it adds no route choice; a unique action may be valuable because it marks a dramatic moment rather than becoming a reusable system.

## Inspect roles against their relationship

Define the intended fairness or contribution: matched winning chances, chosen disadvantage, reciprocal support, or worthwhile participation. Compare each role's opportunities, information, choices, downtime, recovery, and social dependence. Equal button counts and pooled team completion cannot establish equal agency or satisfaction.

A quiet lookout may be welcome to a player choosing low activity and frustrating to someone expecting constant decisions. Keep both accounts. Record role selection, familiarity, partner, map, and relevant access context. Rotating roles can help inspect role effects but introduces learning and order effects. Never infer a universal balance adjustment from an uncontrolled outcome split.

## Choose a prototype by the uncertainty

Write the pending decision, competing explanations, required fidelity, and observation that could change the decision. A movable paper map can test predicted connectivity, but cannot establish camera comfort or tactile responsiveness. A short audiovisual slice may be the smallest valid prototype when timing, sound, or motion carries the experience. A narrated demonstration tests comprehension with that instruction, not unaided discovery.

Record build, participant context, prior exposure, assistance, and accommodations. Separate events from interpretation: repeated attempts can reflect interest, confusion, or an execution barrier. Pair observation with neutral questions. Think-aloud can help inspect mental models but can disrupt tension, comic timing, and execution; uninterrupted play followed by replay questions is another option. Facilitation belongs in a test when it is part of ordinary play.

Keep private responses before group discussion when peer influence is a concern. Retain role-level differences. Do not impose a universal sample size or complaint threshold: one reproducible rule contradiction can establish a defect; an experience hypothesis needs suitable participant evidence. A frequent complaint is not automatically an independent confirmation, and an uncommon participation blocker can still matter.

Changing several things at once can be a valid production repair. It does not isolate which change caused an outcome. Preserve that distinction instead of forbidding useful iteration.

## Exercise and explained answer

Synthetic scenario: after brighter flags, a slower wind cycle, and a bypass were added, more travelers crossed the bridge. The team says brighter flags taught timing. What should the review conclude?

The bundle may have improved crossing, but neither the cause nor timing learning follows from completion alone. Record which route and action each traveler used and whether prior exposure differs. To study the cue specifically, compare appropriate first encounters with other relevant conditions held stable. To study transfer, provide a fresh timing opportunity that cannot be completed solely through the bypass. If open exploration is the real objective, crossing by the bypass may already be a success; do not replace that objective with compulsory mastery.
