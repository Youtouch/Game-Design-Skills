# Reason about space, information, and returns

Use the relevant lesson for the pending decision. These are original teaching examples, not observed player results. [Source notes](spatial-sources.md) distinguish the craft foundations from our proposed procedures.

Before making a spatial recommendation, identify which claim it addresses:

| Claim | What can establish it |
|---|---|
| A destination or return is reachable | A directed trace with the relevant state, prerequisites, and resources |
| The moving entity can traverse a connection | A capability model with appropriate geometry and dynamics, or a representative prototype |
| A cue is available | Viewpoint, occlusion, presentation, and channel checks |
| Players infer a destination or action | Evidence about interpretation; availability alone is insufficient |
| The route feels comfortable or worthwhile | Contextual observation and participant accounts, with rival explanations |

## 1. Trace the return in the changed state

**Purpose and mechanism.** Find traps that a connected-looking map conceals. A visit can consume an ability, close a passage, or change the world. The return must work in the resulting state, not just on the original diagram.

**Procedure.** Label places by decisions or opportunities. Record directed connections, preconditions, costs, irreversible changes, and reset rules. Trace an intended route and a detour, checking completion or recovery after irreversible actions. Attach the actual entity's capability assumptions; use symbolic dimensions when unknown. Exhaustive claims require a complete model, not a few sampled paths.

**Synthetic example.** A maintenance capsule visits dock D, junction J, and inspection chamber C. D↔J is unrestricted. J→C passes through a 0.40 m slot; C→D through a 0.30 m slot. The capsule is 0.35 m wide, C→J is unavailable, and there is no reset. Under a width-only model, entering C strands it. Widening the exit to 0.40 m removes that width contradiction. It does not establish that an unspecified bend permits turning.

**Counterexample and exception.** Widening a passage cannot repair a missing key prerequisite. Conversely, a one-way route can serve commitment if completion or the intended ending remains available; returning to the start is not a universal requirement.

**Exercise.** An inspection action at C consumes the battery needed by C→D. The player can enter C with one battery and must inspect before leaving. Does the visible return passage prove recovery?

**Answer.** No. After inspection the battery state is zero. A free return, battery supply, or another explicitly added transition could repair the model. Check the intended commitment before choosing. A clearer passage cue cannot restore the missing resource.

## 2. Carry orientation across an occluding turn

**Purpose and mechanism.** Give a route choice an interpretable basis without revealing its secrets. A distant landmark can orient an outward journey but disappear behind geometry or mean something different on return.

**Procedure.** At consequential choices, record available evidence and the expected inference: destination, hazard, question, or optional invitation. Check outward and reverse views, display scale, and sensory channels. Where the anchor disappears, identify a local cue that continues the relationship and a way to confirm arrival. Test expected destinations separately from route completion.

**Synthetic example.** A chart-guided skiff sees a split-roof observatory before entering reeds. Matching split marks on junction buoys carry that relation after the building disappears. The return faces instead show the harbor emblem. A selectable chart description reports the same visible marks and directions. It does not declare which destination the player ought to choose.

**Counterexample and exception.** Unique sculptures at every fork need not help if none expresses a stable relation. A hidden inlet may intentionally have little invitation; a decoy may support a mystery. State what is meant to remain unknown and how mistaken expectations can be revised.

**Exercise.** The building is visible from the start, but both passages at the next fork look identical. Should the repair reveal the building's secret?

**Answer.** No. A route-specific mark, view, or equivalent description can connect a passage to the destination without revealing what happens there. Ask players what they expect at the fork and why. That investigates interpretation; it does not replace a separate return-path check.

## 3. Trace evidence encounter orders

**Purpose and mechanism.** Check a deduction without equating arrival with understanding. Movement determines where inspection is possible; an evidence map identifies what each inspection can establish. A clue may be reachable yet unnoticed, inaccessible through one channel, or insufficient until another clue is encountered.

**Procedure.** State the intended inference and a plausible rival. Map relevant observations to places, views, controls, and channel equivalents. Trace several allowed encounter orders and revisits. Check whether the evidence actually supports a unique inference at that point. Use text/cards to inspect relationships, then restore presentation, travel, memory, and interruption demands before predicting full-level play.

**Synthetic example.** In a point-and-click warehouse, a record identifies a parcel by a triangular seal beside a torn label. The front view of parcel A shows only the label; a side inspection reveals the seal. B and C can be fully inspected and each lacks one required feature. A narrated inspection can describe the triangle and tear at the appropriate views. Naming A as the answer provides a stronger hint than equivalent evidence. If B's hidden side has never been inspected, the claim that it lacks a seal is not established.

**Counterexample and exception.** Enlarging the inspection button does not resolve a record that names two incompatible parcel identifiers. A mystery can also remain deliberately underdetermined until a later visit; do not invent uniqueness to complete the table.

**Exercise.** An observation log records that parcel A has a torn label. Should it automatically mark A as the solution?

**Answer.** Not if combining the label and seal is the intended inference. Record observations and locations first. An optional solution hint can be offered as a distinct aid. Check whether both features are obtainable and understandable before claiming either version supports the desired deduction.

## 4. Separate route pacing from a preferred chronology

**Purpose and mechanism.** Make a beat chart work for exploration and revisits. The same place can introduce a question on one route and answer it on another. Transit time does not specify the meaning of that interval.

**Procedure.** Attach beats to places, events, or state changes. Distinguish prerequisites from preferred order. Trace direct, exploratory, and return paths; estimate transit separately from stops. Note changed knowledge, views, shortcuts, or deliberate repetition on return. Keep demand dimensions distinct instead of hiding them in one total.

**Synthetic example.** A floating lantern follows a 150 m channel at an assumed 3 m/s in still conditions: fifty seconds of uninterrupted transit. One route visits a listening pool before a ridge, another after it. The pool teaches no required control, so visiting the ridge first is not automatically a learning failure. A 60 m return shortcut takes twenty modeled seconds and exposes a new view of the pool. Wind, steering, and voluntary stops remain outside these estimates.

**Counterexample and exception.** Adding a reward every few seconds can interrupt reflection while leaving a confusing fork unresolved. Repeating an unchanged road may be the desired ritual; a shortcut's value can differ between first and later visits.

**Exercise.** A branch skips the only opportunity to acquire an ability required at the destination. Another branch offers a long optional quiet shore. Do both fail the pacing chart?

**Answer.** The first requires a prerequisite trace: can the ability be obtained elsewhere or at the destination, or is there a real trap? The shore's duration and low demand do not establish a defect. Investigate intent and experience. Enjoying the shore does not repair missing ability access, and proving access does not establish enjoyable travel.

## 5. Make deliberate disorientation inspectable

**Purpose and mechanism.** Preserve a misleading space while distinguishing intended interpretation from inaccessible evidence or accidental trapping. Deduction benefits from actions that discriminate between spatial hypotheses; a surreal piece may instead choose unresolved contradiction.

**Procedure.** Write the expected initial mistake, the actual transition rule, and evidence that can revise the mistake. Trace the misleading route and recovery. Separate interpretive uncertainty from sensory exclusion, input precision, and motion discomfort. Consider assistance in distinct levels: repeat observations, orient toward a known place, explain an inference. Identify what each changes.

**Synthetic example.** A gallery's west arch appears to lead outside. Crossing it returns to the lobby when a brass dial points to winter; with the dial at summer it reaches a courtyard. A returning lobby emblem and unchanged dial provide evidence of the rule. A reset returns to the lobby without erasing notes. A spatial description can identify the emblem and dial state; an optional hint explains their relation. A comfortable camera option addresses another demand. Their effectiveness remains to be tested.

**Counterexample and exception.** Random destination changes with no discriminating evidence cannot support this particular rule-deduction goal. Unresolved impossible space may nevertheless fit a surreal walk. Judge that choice against its own purpose, costs, access, and recovery rather than demanding a solvable puzzle.

**Exercise.** The only repeated-place cue is a sound that one participant cannot hear. Must the loop be removed?

**Answer.** Equivalent perceivable evidence can preserve the loop and the inference. Describing the repeated sound's location/state differs from announcing the loop's solution. Either may be selectable support, with different effects. Trace the loop's rule and investigate cue interpretation and comfort separately; confusion alone proves neither artistic success nor failure.
